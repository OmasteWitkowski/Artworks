This Rose Looks Cast in Gold. It Isn't.

Ember Rose is a single rose, head-on, that glows like embossed gold and copper in a grid of speckled, cracked panels.

You have the sofa you wanted, and the paint is settled. Above it sits a wide, bare stretch of wall that has bothered you for months. You have looked. You have scrolled through hundreds of pictures, and most of them look like everything else: soft bouquets, tidy prints, the one you nearly bought and then saw in someone else's hallway.

Somewhere in that looking, you began to wonder whether you are simply not an art person. You are. You just haven't been stopped yet. Then a rose appears that does not look like a rose picture at all. It looks as if it were cast in glowing gold, with bright ridges on every petal and a dark center pulling your eye in.

What I was holding when I made it

This rose came from a bouquet given to me by someone special. I dried the flowers and set them in a glass crystal candle holder. Then I backlit the glass and took a photo. What stopped me was the way the angles of the crystal caught the light.

I made it at a hard time. My mother had a terminal diagnosis, and I was struggling with love, life and immortality. Shining light through crystal into dried flowers is how I got the glow, and the squares, bubbles and cracks around the rose belong to that same light.

What I hope you notice, after living with it a while, is that it is real and unreal at the same time. A golden memory that wasn't actually what you thought it was. But whatever is?

I won't tell you what the rose means. You may see heat, or richness, or simply a color you love. Your own response is the only test that counts.

What your eye finds when it stays

  • Bright, raised petal ridges that look embossed. They catch the light like polished metal and give the rose real visual weight, nothing like a soft bouquet.
  • Molten gold, burnt orange and deep rust red, with dark brown shadow and pale cream highlights. The warm, glowing palette lifts a room that needs heat.
  • Petals curling inward toward a dark center. Your eye is drawn in and keeps traveling, so the picture holds attention each time you pass it.
  • Soft folds sweeping out to the right and upper right. They give the metal a slow, liquid feel, as if it were still moving.
  • A grid of square panels behind the rose, with a lobed shape in the upper right. The tiles give the flower a frame and make a familiar subject feel unexpected.
  • Fine bubble-like rings and thin cracks, mostly on the left and lower panels. Walk up close and the speckled, aged surface rewards you. Step back and the rose reads as one bold shape.

The wall it was made for

The rose sits slightly left of center and fills most of a horizontal frame, so this is a wide-wall picture. Think of the space above a sofa, a sideboard, a bed or a fireplace. The large single flower, strong contrast and saturated warm color hold up from across the room.

It suits a living room, dining room, library or entry that needs a focal point and some glow. The mood is warm, rich and a little antique. It is a piece for a room where you want presence, not quiet background.
