A white ripple, a blue pool, and a path to the snow

Pacific Northwest Landscapes · horizontal

"Where the Water Slows, Spring Meadow and Snowy Peak" is a wide landscape photograph that leads your eye up a stream to a snow-streaked mountain.

You have a wide wall. It might be above the sofa, the bed or the console. The furniture is settled and the paint is done, and that one stretch of wall still looks unfinished every time you walk past.

This is the picture I would hold up to it. A narrow stream spills in white ripples over a low ledge, then smooths into a blue pool that mirrors a cloudless sky. From there the water winds off through bright green meadow toward the snow.

I can't give you a tall tale about this picture, and I won't make one up. What I can give you is what happens when you look at it, because the picture is built to be read from the bottom up.

First comes the ripple. White water crosses the stream at a small ledge of sticks and debris, and it is the brightest, busiest spot in the frame. Just behind it the surface goes smooth and holds the blue of the sky.

From the pool the stream curves along the center, through the green meadow, and stops at a dark band of evergreens. Above the trees a slate-gray ridge carries streaks of snow, with a big open sky over it all.

Nothing in the picture is hazy or hidden, and I like that. The light is crisp and even, the colors are strong but natural, and the view is easy to take in. It also rewards a second look.

The wide horizontal format is made for a wide wall: above a sofa, a bed, a mantel or a console, or down a hallway or office wall. The bands of sky, peak, forest, meadow and water stay readable from a distance, so it works in a room you see from the doorway as well as from the couch.

The palette is sky blue, fresh spring green and deep conifer green, with white, slate and a little dusty orange. It suits rooms with neutral walls, wood tones, or other blues and greens. The big open sky gives a larger piece an airy feel.

I don't think there's a test except your own reaction. If the picture stops you and you can picture it on your wall, that is reason enough. There is no right or wrong answer, and you don't need to explain it to anyone.
